Today's question: if a bottle of oil is mislabeled (say, labeled “extra virgin olive oil” but actually blended with refined olive oil or another oil), can spectra + PCA reveal it? (We'll come back to this at the score plot — but you'll also see that PCA can sometimes be fooled by a single outlier.)

Principle

What PCA looks for: the direction of greatest variance

  • PC1: the direction of greatest variance in the data; PC2: the direction perpendicular to PC1 with the next-greatest variance — the two are orthogonal to each other (⟂)
  • PCA is an unsupervised method — it has no idea which oil a sample is and separates samples using only the variance in the spectra
  • So if different oils really separate on the score plot, it means the input spectra differ; but whether the difference truly comes from chemical composition still has to be confirmed with the loadings, the raw spectra and the measurement conditions — and you'll shortly see that the difference can also be misled by a single outlier
Principle

Score = weighted sum of the loadings

t = p₁(x₁−x̄₁) + p₂(x₂−x̄₂) + p₃(x₃−x̄₃) + …

t = the sample's score on this PC; pⱼ = the loading (weight) of wavelength j; xⱼ−x̄ⱼ = the mean-centered value at that wavelength

p=[0.6, 0.6, 0.53], centered x=[0.5, 0.4, 0.3]
t = 0.6×0.5 + 0.6×0.4 + 0.53×0.3 = 0.30+0.24+0.159 ≈ 0.70
  • Loading: the “weight” of each wavelength on this PC — it determines “why” the samples separate
  • Score: multiply each wavelength's (mean-centered) signal by its weight and add them up — this is the sample's coordinate on the plot
  • The larger the loading (positive or negative), the more that wavelength influences this PC; a loading near 0 means that wavelength barely affects this PC

Preprocessing · work one out

Work through mean-centering and autoscaling once

WavelengthRaw value xx − x̄ (mean-centered)÷ SD (autoscaled)
Signal wavelength0.8 / 1.0 / 1.2−0.2 / 0 / 0.2−1 / 0 / 1
Noise wavelength1.001 / 0.999 / 1.0000.001 / −0.001 / 01 / −1 / 0

Both rows use the sample standard deviation (SD) (calculated as in W01 ↗; signal row SD=0.2, noise row SD=0.001).

  • Mean-centering: subtract each row's own mean (1.0) from its three values — so PCA looks for variance “relative to the mean spectrum”; this preprocessing is almost always required
  • Autoscaling: divide by each row's own standard deviation after centering — the signal wavelength and the noise wavelength both end up on the same −1/0/1 scale
  • Here is the problem: the noise wavelength originally had a tiny signal (SD=0.001), yet after dividing it looks just as “prominent” as the signal wavelength — the noise has been amplified, which is why spectral data usually skips autoscaling
Preprocessing options

SNV / derivatives: whether to apply them before PCA

📐
SNV (standard normal variate)
Do an SNV first (see W11 ↗): it removes the intercept/slope differences caused by particle-size scattering, so the variance PCA finds is closer to real chemical differences
📉
First / second derivative
Do a derivative first (see W11 ↗): it removes baseline drift, but also amplifies high-frequency noise — think twice before using it
🧪
Today's demo: mean-centering only
Today's score plot and loadings come from PCA on the raw intensities with mean-centering only — no SNV or derivatives; we leave that comparison for the assignment
This demo uses mean-centering only, no autoscaling; in the Orange PCA widget, remember to uncheck “Normalize variables” (it is on by default).
Principle

Eigenvalue and explained variance

λₐ = variance of the scores on the a-th PC (eigenvalue)
Explained variance % = λₐ / Σλᵢ × 100%

The larger λₐ is, the more spread out the samples are along the a-th PC — and the more “important” that PC is.

  • Today's six-oil data: PC1 ≈ 46.3%, PC2 ≈ 28.3%, PC3 ≈ 23.0%, and everything from PC4 on adds up to only 0.53%
  • Scree plot: a bar chart of the explained variance of each PC, used to decide “how many PCs are enough to look at”
  • ⚠️ Don’t confuse this λ with the λ (smoothness parameter) in W11 ↗ ALS baseline correction — they are two completely different things that just happen to share the Greek letter λ

Scree plot: explained variance falls with each PC

Score plot

PC1 vs PC2: score plot of six oils

  • PC1 (46.3%): driven mainly by 1655/1265 cm⁻¹ (unsaturation-related) — coconut oil (the most saturated) sits at the most negative end
  • PC2 (28.3%): it looks as if extra virgin olive oil separates, but a sum-of-squares check shows that 74% of the PC2 variance comes from one single point, peanut oil replicate 3; its loading peak near 1159 cm⁻¹ is an artifact left by a measurement anomaly, not a clean chemical signal
  • QC: the replicates of five oils cluster tightly; one peanut oil replicate sits far at the top — suspect the measurement first (air bubble? alignment?), and don't rush to say “peanut oil forms its own group”
  • Back to the opening question: whether PCA can catch a mislabeled oil depends first on making sure the PC you are looking at is not hijacked by a single outlier — on the next page we remove this point and redo the analysis

The data come from the same simulated Raman spectra used in Colab NB10 (nb10_demo_spectra.csv); the PCA was computed live when this page was built, so the numbers are guaranteed to match NB10. Point color = oil; three points of the same color = three replicates.

Hands-on: redo it

Redo the PCA after removing PEA-3

  • With peanut oil replicate 3 removed: PC1 = 63.5%, PC2 = 33.2% (higher than the 28.3% before removal — an increase, not a decrease), PC3 is down to just 0.74% — the 74% of PC2 sum of squares that PEA-3 alone had propped up is gone, and the remaining variance re-concentrates into one clean direction
  • Now the highest PC2 loading is near 1525 cm⁻¹ = the carotenoid band: extra virgin olive oil (EVOO) has a mean PC2 score of 0.99, while all the other oils lie between −0.39 and 0.03 — this is the “clean” carotenoid separation
  • Lesson: a single outlier can hijack an entire PC. When you find an outlier sample, first go back to the raw spectrum and record the reason (see W08 ↗), then redo the PCA to check whether the story changes
  • NB10 preview: this point’s Hotelling T² = 12.7, above the 95% limit of 7.7 — the formal definitions of T² / Q residual (SPE) are left for W14 ↗

Data · Loadings

PC1 loading: which wavenumbers drive this principal component

The PC1 loading has positive peaks near 1655 cm⁻¹ (C=C stretching) and near 1265 cm⁻¹ (=C–H bending) — both are Raman bands related to unsaturation, which also explains why coconut oil (highly saturated) is farthest away on PC1. Treat band assignments as approximate; real samples still need to be confirmed against standards or literature spectra.
What the signs mean: for a band with a positive loading, samples with higher intensity there also have higher scores on this PC; for a negative loading, a stronger signal gives a lower score. What if two groups overlap: look at PC3, or try a different preprocessing (such as the second derivative).
Orange OR12 step by step

How to click from spectra to score plot and loadings

  • File: set oil as the target; set sample_id / group / instrument / rep / oil_zh as meta — leave the numeric wavenumber columns as they are, as features
  • PCA: uncheck “Normalize variables” (spectra get mean-centering only); the default of 2 components gives PC1+PC2 — to see more (e.g., PC3), increase the “Components” number
  • Connect PCA's Data output (not Transformed Data) to Scatter Plot: x=PC1, y=PC2, Color=oil
  • Connect PCA's Components output to Spectra: plot loading vs wavenumber (wavelength)
  • In Scatter Plot, select the odd-looking points → check the raw values in Data Table (this is how PEA-3 was caught)

Orange workflow

From spectra to score plot and loadings

The Colab / R code below is an advanced option — you can finish today's analysis with the Orange steps on the previous page, no coding needed.

Advanced: Colab (scikit-learn, 5 lines):

from sklearn.decomposition import PCA import numpy as np X = np.loadtxt("oils_X.csv", delimiter=",") # one spectrum (one sample) per row pca = PCA(n_components=5).fit(X - X.mean(axis=0)) scores = pca.transform(X - X.mean(axis=0)) # scores[:, :2] -> score plot

Advanced: R (prcomp):

X <- as.matrix(read.csv("oils_X.csv", header=FALSE)) pca <- prcomp(X, center=TRUE, scale.=FALSE) scores <- pca$x[, 1:2] loadings <- pca$rotation[, 1] summary(pca)$importance[2, ] # proportion of variance explained by each PC

Samples, instruments and safety

Six edible oils × Raman 785 + each group's NIR

  • Six oils: extra virgin olive oil, refined olive oil, soybean oil, canola oil, coconut oil, peanut oil — every group measures all of them, each with 3 replicates (re-loaded / re-sampled)
  • Raman 785 nm (rotating station): extra virgin olive oil gives strong fluorescence interference at 532 nm — remember the lesson from W10; today we use 785 nm throughout
  • Each group's NIR: depending on your group, use one of Avantes (300–1100 / 900–1650 nm; 600–1100 nm awaiting delivery), InnoSpectra NIR-S-G1 / T1 / T11 / R14, NeoSpectra Scanner / Micro Dev Kit / fiber-optic module, or Spectral Engines NIRONE S2.0 / S2.2 / S2.5
  • Coconut oil: it solidifies at room temperature (below about 24°C), so warm it gently until melted into a liquid before measuring
🔴
Raman laser (532/785 nm)
Never look directly at the light source or aim the beam at anyone's eyes; wear appropriate eye protection as instructed for the instrument
🔥🛢️
Hot oil and slippery floors
Warm coconut oil only gently, just until liquid — do not overheat; wipe up any oil spill right away to avoid slipping
