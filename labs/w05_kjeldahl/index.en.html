Key question for today: The Kjeldahl method (like the Dumas combustion method we will mention later) only measures “total nitrogen”, then uses a conversion factor to turn nitrogen into “protein”. When does this assumption go wrong? The melamine scandal is the most famous case.

Work out one number and keep it in mind: Melamine is ≈ 66.6% N. Mixing in 1 g per 100 g of sample inflates the apparent protein (total N × factor, with non-protein nitrogen not subtracted; using the dairy factor 6.38) by about 4.25 percentage points. We will recalculate it together shortly.

Protein = %N × conversion factor

The Kjeldahl method measures “total nitrogen”, not “protein”

Conversion factorApplies toReason (brief)
6.25General foods (default)Assumes protein averages 16% nitrogen (1/0.16 = 6.25)
5.7Wheat / cerealsCereal gluten proteins have a higher nitrogen fraction, so a smaller factor is needed
6.38DairyMilk proteins such as casein have a lower nitrogen fraction, so a larger factor is needed
The Kjeldahl method (like the Dumas combustion method) measures “total nitrogen”, which includes protein nitrogen and also non-protein nitrogen (NPN), such as melamine, urea, free amino acids and nucleic acids. The conversion factor is an approximation that “assumes all nitrogen comes from protein”. Once a sample contains a nitrogen-rich non-protein substance, the calculated “protein %” is inflated.
Dumas combustion method: the sample is fully combusted at high temperature and the nitrogen gas is measured directly. It is faster than Kjeldahl (minutes vs hours), but it still measures total nitrogen and is still fooled by NPN. We do not run it in this lab; it is included for comparison only.
Note: the 5.7 factor for wheat/cereals is the long-standing traditional factor from Jones/Codex.

The melamine scandal: why was the Kjeldahl method fooled?

Melamine (C₃H₆N₆): MW = 126.12, nitrogen content as high as 66.63%

  • In normal milk, almost all nitrogen comes from milk proteins such as casein (protein nitrogen)
  • Unscrupulous producers deliberately added melamine to milk powder that had been diluted with water. It is not protein, but its nitrogen content is very high
  • The Kjeldahl method only sees “total nitrogen” and cannot tell whether a nitrogen came from protein or from melamine
Let’s calculate: for every 1 g melamine / 100 g sample (i.e., 1% w/w):
Contribution to %N = 1% × 66.63% ≈ 0.6663 percentage points
Using the dairy factor 6.38, the inflated apparent protein ≈ 0.6663 × 6.38 ≈ 4.25 percentage points

In other words, adding only about 1% melamine raises the Kjeldahl “protein %” by ~4.3 percentage points. That is enough to disguise watered-down milk powder with too little protein as “passing”.

Calibration and quality control (QC)

4 things to confirm before and during the run

⚗️
HCl standardization
The HCl used for titration must first be standardized against a primary standard such as Na₂CO₃ or THAM (Tris) to get its exact concentration before it can be used to calculate %N
🧪
Reagent blank
An empty tube goes through the whole digestion + distillation + titration procedure (no sample) to subtract the trace nitrogen introduced by the reagents themselves
♻️
Distillation recovery standard
Run ammonium sulfate of known nitrogen content (theoretical %N = 21.20%) through distillation + titration to check whether NH₃ is fully distilled out and trapped
🔬
Digestion efficiency standard
Run an organic nitrogen standard such as lysine hydrochloride or tryptophan through the whole procedure to check whether digestion fully converts the organic nitrogen
Calibration and quality control (QC)

Let’s calculate: recovery and end-point reading

Let’s calculate (distillation recovery): Weigh 0.0500 g ammonium sulfate (theoretical %N = 21.20%); distillation + titration gives %N = 21.01%, so recovery = 21.01/21.20×100 ≈ 99.1% (AOAC 991.20: ammonium sulfate ≥99%, lysine hydrochloride ≥98%).
Let’s calculate (digestion efficiency): Lysine hydrochloride has a theoretical %N of 15.34%; the full procedure gives %N = 15.10%, so recovery ≈ 98.4%. This semester the TA demonstrates one tube of these two standards so the whole class understands the principle; they are not in the 8-tube group schedule below.
Consistent indicator end point: The whole class uses the same batch of 4% boric acid + mixed indicator and must read the same end point: after absorbing NH₃ the receiving solution is green; titrate with HCl until the green fades to grayish purple / pale pink, which is the end point. This avoids differences in interpretation between groups.
Samples and station rotation

Safety rules

Conc. sulfuric acid · NaOH · Waste

🥽
Hot conc. sulfuric acid + white fumes
Digestion produces irritating SO₂/SO₃ gases. Work in the fume hood/scrubber throughout; wear a face shield and acid-resistant gloves
💧
Dilute by adding water slowly down the tube wall
Only after cooling to room temperature, add water slowly and in small amounts down the tube wall. Never add a lot of water quickly, or the violent heat release will splatter and burn
⚠️
40% NaOH is strongly corrosive
The NaOH added for distillation is highly concentrated. If it gets on skin or eyes, rinse immediately with plenty of water and tell the TA
♻️
Collect copper catalyst waste separately
Copper-containing waste after digestion is heavy-metal waste and must be collected separately as required; do not mix it with general waste

Calculation: from titration volume to % protein

Subtract the blank first, then multiply by the conversion factor

%N = (Vsample − Vblank) × NHCl × 14.007Wsample(g) × 1000 × 100 Protein % = %N × conversion factor

Each 1 mmol HCl ↔ 1 mmol NH₃ ↔ 1 mmol N = 14.007 mg N; ÷(W×1000) converts mg to g, and ×100 gives %.

Whole milk powder (blind sample) example: 0.5000 g weighed, blank 0.30 mL, titrant used 14.86 mL, 0.1000 N HCl
%N = (14.86−0.30)×0.1000×14.007/(0.5000×1000)×100 ≈ 4.079%
Protein % = 4.079 × 6.38 ≈ 26.02% (dairy factor)
Bread flour example (the tube shared by the whole class): 1.0000 g weighed, blank 0.30 mL, titrant used 16.58 mL, 0.1000 N HCl
%N ≈ 2.280%; protein % (factor 5.7) = 2.280×5.7 ≈ 13.00%
⚠️ The cost of the wrong factor: For the same bread flour %N, using the general factor 6.25 instead of the wheat factor 5.7 changes the protein % from 13.00% to 14.25%, which is about 9.6% too high (relative error). This is a systematic error. Like melamine, it comes from the assumption that “nitrogen equals protein”, only for a different reason (wrong factor vs adulterated sample).
Cake flour (low-gluten) has %N ≈ 1.404% and protein % (factor 5.7) ≈ 8.00%. This time it is provided by the TA (data from a previous run); our class did not run this tube. It is lower than bread flour, which is why “bread flour makes bread and cake flour makes cake” in baking.
