Principle 1

Refractometer Brix: refractive index ↔ soluble solids

Definition: The Brix scale relates refractive index at 20°C to “sucrose % (w/w).” Besides sugar, juices and syrups contain acids, salts and other solutes that also raise the refractive index — so a sample's °Brix ≈ total soluble solids, not exactly “pure sugar content.” This is also why we compare orange juice Brix with phenol–sulfuric total sugar later.
Temperature compensation: Most modern handheld/benchtop refractometers have ATC (automatic temperature compensation), but you should still work within the recommended temperature range.
Extension: non-destructive Brix

ATAGO PAL-HIKARi: read Brix without cutting the fruit

  • An infrared LED shines light into the fruit and the detector picks up light scattered back from the flesh — no cutting or juicing; measure on the tree or after harvest
  • The instrument has built-in conversion models for each fruit, so models are sold by fruit type (grape, strawberry, apple, pear, mango, melon, watermelon, corn… over 25 kinds) and users need no calibration of their own
  • The reading is a model-estimated Brix; such models are generally built with refractometer Brix of juice as the reference, so accuracy still has to be checked against the refractometer
  • Strong outdoor sunlight interferes; the display shows “nnn” to prompt you to shade the fruit and remeasure (ambient-light interference detected)
The manufacturer's webpage does not publish the wavelengths used or the accuracy specification; however ATAGO's US patent US11099127 discloses a design for a handheld non-destructive sugar meter, which we take apart on the next page (a patent design is not necessarily the specification of this product). Extension: taking a handheld sugar meter apart

ATAGO patent US11099127: how the light travels

  • Light path: LED light enters the flesh from the outer ring through the ring lens; the scattered light is collected by the central light guide and passes through the filters to the sensor — illumination and collection are separated, which blocks direct reflection from the skin surface
  • The 875 nm filter is paired with an 880 nm LED; the 1050 and 1064 nm filters share a 1064 nm LED — LED wavelength ≠ filter wavelength
The patent does not disclose the regression coefficients b0–b8 or any accuracy figures, so it cannot be used to rebuild or evaluate product performance; it is “one design,” not the actual specification of the PAL-HIKARi. Original text on Google Patents ↗
  • Bandpass filter: lets only a narrow band of wavelengths through, so the sensor measures one wavelength at a time

Extension: full-spectrum handheld NIR

Felix F-750: inside is a Carl Zeiss MMS-1 spectrometer

  • Spectrometer: Zeiss MMS-1 (enhanced VIS–NIR version), 310–1100 nm continuous spectrum, 256-pixel silicon photodiode array, about 3 nm sampling, resolution about 10 nm
  • Why it is stable: the grating, fiber inlet and detector are all fixed on a single glass body, aligned at the factory; thermal drift < 0.01 nm/K, suitable for taking to the orchard
  • Measurement: a tungsten lamp shines broadband light on a ring-shaped area of the skin, and after scattering in the flesh it is collected in the center (interactance) — the same concept as the ATAGO patent on the previous page
  • Predicts: soluble solids (°Brix), dry matter (DM), flesh and skin color, titratable acidity
  • You build the model: measure 10–200 training fruit plus destructive reference values (refractometer Brix, oven-dried DM), then use the manufacturer's Model Builder to build a model with PLS (second-derivative absorbance spectra) — exactly what W10–W13 do
  • Common in research: Walsh's group at CQU in Australia built fruit sugar models with the MMS-1 as early as 2000; more recently the F-750 measured mango dry matter with an RMSEP of about 0.8 %FW (Walsh et al., 2024; this is dry matter, not °Brix)
  • Note: the F-751 series (dedicated mango, avocado, etc. units) uses a Hamamatsu 640–1050 nm spectrometer with factory built-in models and is not the MMS-1
The manufacturer claims a prediction error RMSEP of about ±0.5 °Brix (see the “Understanding validation metrics” page for what RMSEP is); this is the vendor's own figure, and a new cultivar or season requires revalidation. Our department's Avantes 300–1100 nm covers almost the same band as the MMS-1 (310–1100 nm), so you can compare your own PLS results against it.

Extension activity

Use this course's NIR instruments to verify non-destructive Brix yourself

Class extension activity: take 10 fruit of one kind (e.g., apples or oranges) and mark 2 spots on each at the equator → measure with both instruments: Avantes 300–1100 nm (fiber reflectance probe) and InnoSpectra NIR-S-G1 (900–1700 nm, placed on the skin), 3 replicates per spot → finish all NIR measurements before cutting the fruit, then cut flesh from the same spot, juice it, and measure Brix with the refractometer as the reference → first see which wavelengths correlate with Brix; in W13 use this data to build a PLS model and compare the two instruments. In general, short-wave NIR penetrates deeper into the flesh, while longer wavelengths mostly see only the part near the skin — which is exactly what makes the two instruments worth comparing.

Understanding validation metrics

Bias, MAE, RMSEP: how far off are the predictions

AbbreviationNameFormulaIn plain words
BiasBiasΣ(ŷ − y) / nWhether predictions are too high or too low on average (positive = overestimate); positive and negative errors cancel, so Bias ≈ 0 does not mean accurate
MAEMean absolute error
MAE		Σ|ŷ − y| / nIgnoring sign, the average miss per fruit in °Brix; the most intuitive
RMSEPRoot mean square error of prediction
RMSEP		√[ Σ(ŷ − y)² / n ]Square, average, then take the square root, so large errors are amplified; also in °Brix, and the one most often reported in papers and by manufacturers

y = refractometer reference value, ŷ = NIR predicted value, n = number of independent fruit. The same formula is called RMSEC when computed on the training set, RMSECV with cross-validation, and RMSEP only when computed on a test set that took no part in model building. Other abbreviations: NIR = near-infrared, PLS = partial least squares regression, DM = dry matter.

A small worked example with 3 fruit (illustrative data): reference 10.0 / 12.0 / 14.0, predicted 10.5 / 11.5 / 14.6 °Brix → error e = ŷ − y = +0.5 / -0.5 / +0.6
Bias = +0.20 MAE = 0.53 RMSEP = 0.54 °Brix
Bias is only +0.20 because +0.5 and −0.5 cancel; every fruit is actually off by about half a degree, so you need MAE and RMSEP to see that.

Principle 2

Phenol–sulfuric acid method (DuBois, 1956): colorimetric determination of total sugar

  • Concentrated sulfuric acid causes sugars to undergo hydrolysis + dehydration, forming furfural derivatives
  • These derivatives condense with phenol to give a stable orange-yellow product, read colorimetrically at 490 nm
  • Measures total sugar (total carbohydrate), including sucrose and oligosaccharides (unlike copper-reduction methods, which measure only reducing sugars); note this is not the “sugars” on a nutrition label, which count only free monosaccharides and disaccharides
  • Sugar alcohols do not react (sorbitol, erythritol, etc.) — the g3 game will verify this shortly
  • Different sugars (e.g., fructose vs glucose) have different molar color yields, so changing the standard causes systematic bias
  • Color development is not strictly stoichiometric, so you must always run your own calibration curve
  • Add the concentrated sulfuric acid quickly in one go onto the liquid surface: the instant heat release makes the dehydration fast and uniform; adding slowly down the wall tends to layer and gives inconsistent color
Colorimetric determination

From color to concentration: the calibration curve

  • Absorbance A: the darker the color, the more light absorbed and the larger A; within a range A is proportional to concentration (Beer–Lambert: A=εbc)
  • Calibration curve: measure A of standards of known concentration, use linear regression (least squares) to draw the best-fit line A=slope×C+intercept, then back-calculate C from the unknown sample's A
  • R²: the fraction of data variation explained by the line (0–1), but insensitive to “curvature”
  • Residual = measured A − A predicted by the line; it should scatter randomly around 0
  • LOD (limit of detection) = the lowest concentration at which you can reliably say “present”; LOQ (limit of quantitation) = the lowest concentration at which you can reliably report “how much”
  • Molar color yield: each mole of sugar gives a different amount of color, so results are written as “glucose equivalents”

Reducing vs non-reducing sugars

Check whether the anomeric carbon is free

  • Anomeric carbon: the carbon on the sugar ring bonded to two oxygens (glucose C1, fructose C2), originally the aldehyde/ketone carbon
  • Reducing sugar: at least one anomeric carbon is free (hemiacetal/hemiketal, which can open to an aldehyde or ketone) and can reduce Cu²⁺ to Cu₂O: glucose, fructose, lactose, maltose
  • Non-reducing sugar: all anomeric carbons are used in glycosidic bonds: sucrose (C1–C2, head to head), trehalose (C1–C1), raffinose (contains a sucrose unit), cyclodextrin (cyclic, no end)
  • Inversion: cleaving sucrose with acid or invertase → glucose + fructose, so that it can be measured by copper reduction

Calibration

Before starting, calibrate three things

💧
Zero the refractometer
Zero with distilled water at 20°C; then check with a 10.0% (w/w) sucrose standard solution, which should read 10.0 °Brix
⬛
Zero the spectrophotometer
Zero with the reagent blank (no sugar, only phenol + concentrated sulfuric acid) to subtract the absorbance background of the reagents
📈
Run a calibration curve every time
Glucose standards 0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100 µg/mL; prepare a new curve for each batch and never reuse an old one

Safety rules

Concentrated sulfuric acid · phenol · fume hood

🥽
Concentrated sulfuric acid releases intense heat when mixed with water
Wear a face shield + acid-resistant gloves throughout; add the acid quickly onto the liquid surface inside the fume hood, and never add water to acid
☠️
Phenol is toxic and is absorbed through the skin
Wear gloves and avoid skin contact; phenol waste must not be mixed with general waste and must be collected separately
🌡️
Do not hold hot test tubes directly
Tubes are very hot after the water bath; handle them with tube holders and let them cool before using the spectrophotometer
🧴
Collect concentrated sulfuric acid/phenol waste separately
Sort and collect according to the lab SOP; never pour down the sink

QC

The R² myth: a high R² does not mean good regression quality

Residual plot (illustrative data)
  • This group's calibration curve has R² = 0.9994 (looks beautiful), but the residuals are negative at both ends and positive in the middle, forming an arc — a sign that you should check the residuals, not just R²
  • LOD = 3.3σ/slope ≈ 3.50 µg/mL; LOQ = 10σ/slope ≈ 10.62 µg/mL (here σ is the SD of the regression residuals; in practice the SD of multiple blank readings is often used directly, with the same principle)
  • Sample readings below the LOQ can only be qualitative and should not be reported as quantitative results
Samples and data

Four samples, one comparison to discuss

  • Maple syrup: must be diluted heavily first to fall within the calibration range
  • Orange juice: measure both Brix and phenol–sulfuric total sugar and compare them
  • Sports drink: often contains high-fructose corn syrup (HFCS); watch for bias from the choice of standard
  • Winter melon tea: a dark sample; check whether the color after dilution still interferes with colorimetry, and subtract the background color with a sample blank without phenol
Orange juice comparison (illustrative data): Brix ≈ 11.5 °Brix vs phenol–sulfuric total sugar ≈ 9.5 g/100mL (glucose equivalent), a difference of ≈ 2.0. The gap mainly arises because Brix also counts acids and other soluble solids, while the phenol–sulfuric method colors only sugars; differences in color yield between sugars such as fructose and glucose also cause a small discrepancy.
Mass conservation (illustrative calculation, answering the opening 40:1):

Sap 2%, syrup 66% → mass ratio = 66/2 = 33 : 1

The volume ratio needs a density correction:
33 × (syrup density 1.33 / sap density 1.008) ≈ 43.5 : 1

Rule of 86: 86/2% ≈ 43 : 1, consistent with the volume ratio calculated above; the video's “40:1” fits exactly if the sap is just slightly sweeter (about 2.18%) — the order of magnitude matches, so this claim survives a mass-balance check.
