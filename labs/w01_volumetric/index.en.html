Think first
Does the 10 mL pipette in your hand
really deliver 10 mL?

Testing labs must calibrate volumetric glassware regularly under ISO/IEC 17025 (the international accreditation standard for the competence of testing and calibration laboratories). The international standard ISO 4787 specifies how to calibrate glass volumetric ware by the “gravimetric method”.
Today you will verify for yourself whether your own pipette and burette are truly “accurate”.

Principles 2/2

Reading, delivery, and temperature: the fine points

👁️
Parallax
Read the meniscus at eye level; looking from above misjudges the volume
⏱️
Delivery time and touching the wall
Burettes and volumetric pipettes need a fixed waiting time when delivering; finally touch the tip to the flask wall (tip touch-off). For a regular volumetric pipette (TD, To Deliver), the last small drop left in the tip must “not” be blown out — calibration never counts that drop
🌡️
Temperature changes density
Water density varies with temperature; to convert the measured “mass” back to “volume”, you must know the water temperature at the time
🎈
Air buoyancy correction
The balance weighs in air, and the buoyancy on the weights and on the water differs, so a buoyancy correction is needed; the magnitude is about 0.1%

Core procedure

How to do the gravimetric method

  • Volumetric pipette: cap the weighing bottle and tare → draw 10.00 mL of water and deliver it into the bottle → cap and weigh immediately (to 0.1 mg) → record water temperature → repeat 10 times
  • Burette: fill and read the initial level → deliver to 25.00 mL → weigh → repeat 10 times
  • Apparent mass mapp: the number read directly on the balance in air, before air buoyancy is removed; it is converted to volume in the next step

Data processing

From apparent mass to true volume

V = mapp × Fb / ρw
Fb = 1 + ρair(1/ρw − 1/ρweights)

ρair=0.0012 g/mL ρweights=8.0 g/mL ρw is obtained from the Tanaka (2001) equation — the internationally accepted “density–temperature” formula for pure water, from which the table below was calculated.

Example: water delivered from a volumetric pipette has an apparent mass of 9.9700 g at a water temperature of 25.0 °C. ρw(25.0°C) = 0.997047 g/mL, Fb ≈ 1.00105 → V ≈ 10.010 mL.

Data QC

Mean, SD, RSD%, 95% CI, and bias

  • Mean, SD: central tendency and spread of the 10 replicates
  • RSD% = SD/mean × 100: relative spread; the smaller, the more “precise”
  • 95% CI = mean ± t×SD/√n (n=10, t=2.262)
  • bias = mean − nominal value (10.00 or 25.00 mL): the closer to 0, the more “accurate”
  • Significant figures: report the CI to 1–2 significant figures and the mean to the same decimal place

How to read a 95% CI

Significant ≠ failing

📐
SD and t
SD (standard deviation) describes how spread out the data are; t=2.262 is the multiplier from the t table for n=10 (9 degrees of freedom); the smaller n is, the larger t is
🔍
Is the bias significant?
The 95% CI covers the nominal value (the CI of the bias contains 0) → statistically not significant, possibly just random fluctuation; if it does not cover it → significant bias
✅
Does it pass?
Also compare with the Class A tolerance: |bias| ≤ 0.02 mL (pipette). In the figure, student E: the CI contains 0 but the bias exceeds the limit → still needs attention

The two main types of error

Systematic error vs random error

Systematic error (determinate error)Random error (indeterminate error)
DirectionAlways off to the same side (always high or always low)Sometimes positive, sometimes negative, no fixed direction
CauseA specific cause can be found: instrument, method, operating habitMany tiny variations add up; no single cause can be named
Which metric it affectsMean departs from the true value → bias (accuracy)Data scatter → SD, RSD% (precision)
Does repeating cancel it?No; even the average of 100 runs is still offThe mean becomes steadier and the CI narrower, but each run still fluctuates
What to doFind it → calibrate or correct (e.g., buoyancy correction Fb)Increase replicates and describe with SD and 95% CI
The same action can cause either: the waiting time after delivery — if you never wait long enough, the delivered volume is always low → systematic error; if the waiting time varies each time, the delivered volume fluctuates → random error. The key to deciding is “is the direction fixed?”.
A third type of error

Gross error: caught by checking

  • Gross error = misreading, miscopying, mistyping: 10.00 read as 1.000, a misplaced decimal point, a wrong unit, weighing without the cap
  • It is neither systematic nor random error, and statistics cannot “average it out”
  • How to catch it: after entry, compare with your other 9 replicates and with classmates in your group; if it differs a lot, it is suspicious
  • What to do: flag the reason in the note column, keep the original value, and redo it if possible — never delete it silently
  • W8 will teach a formal outlier test (Grubbs)

Class forest plot: see at a glance who is accurate and who is steady

  • Dots = mean, horizontal lines = 95% CI, red dashed line = nominal value 200 µL

Spot the error: what is wrong with this spreadsheet?

📏
Error 1: only 8 values used
The range for mean/SD is written as P2:W2 (8 cells), missing X and Y.
Example: S14's 10th value 0.1970 is left out → RSD shows 0.26% (looks “excellent”); the correct 10-value RSD is 1.38%
🏷️
Error 2: mislabeled column
The “95% CI” column formula is =mean/0.9956, which is just the volume conversion of mass divided by density, unrelated to the confidence interval (and it omits Fb too).
Correct: mean ± 2.262×SD/√10
🎯
Error 3: two errors cancel each other
S11's 7th and 8th values are 0.2487 and 0.1353 (normal is about 0.19). The cumulative column looks smooth, but the individual values swing one high, one low → RSD 14%. This is a gross error: go back to the raw record, flag it, and don't silently delete it
How to prevent it: ① after selecting the range, check the status bar for “Count=10”; ② column titles must match the formulas; ③ a normal cumulative column ≠ normal individual values, so check both columns. Think about it: S11's two extreme values both inflate the SD, so a test based on “how many SDs from the mean” cannot see them — W8 covers the Grubbs test; come back to this example then.
