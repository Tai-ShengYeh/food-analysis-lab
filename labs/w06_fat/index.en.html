Principle

Soxhlet extraction vs why some fat must first be “liberated”

🧪
Soxhlet solvent extraction
A non-polar solvent such as petroleum ether or hexane repeatedly refluxes through a dried, finely ground sample. The solvent carries away the extractable lipids and, once evaporated, leaves them in the flask. What you measure is “crude fat”: besides triglycerides, it may include some phospholipids, pigments, waxes and other fat-soluble components. Dry samples whose fat is not encapsulated, such as oilseeds, nuts and potato chips, can be extracted directly by Soxhlet.
🔓
Why some fat cannot be extracted
In emulsified/encapsulated samples such as dairy, baked goods and mayonnaise, the fat is wrapped by protein or carbohydrate (bound/encapsulated fat) and the solvent cannot reach it, so direct Soxhlet underestimates it. The encapsulating structure must first be broken with acid or alkali (hydrolysis) to “liberate” the fat before extraction.
Samples must be dried and ground fine first: Water is a polar molecule and blocks a non-polar solvent from penetrating the sample, so extracting without drying gives a seriously low result (today we use samples already dried in W2, or pre-dried beforehand). Fine grinding increases the surface area the solvent contacts, making extraction faster and more complete.
Two hydrolysis methods

Liberate the encapsulated fat

🧪
Acid hydrolysis Weibull–Stoldt
Boil in dilute HCl to break the protein/starch encapsulation → filter → dry the residue and then Soxhlet; used for mayonnaise and baked goods (demonstrated by the TA today)
🥛
Alkaline Röse–Gottlieb / Mojonnier
Ammonia dissolves the protein membrane around the fat globules, ethanol helps phase separation, and diethyl ether + petroleum ether extract; the reference method for dairy
📏
Reference method
The method accepted as the most accurate and used to judge the bias of other methods; the W6 blind sample uses it as the benchmark
In class today: potato chips and the blind sample are run by Soxhlet; the mayonnaise acid hydrolysis is demonstrated by the TA and you do not operate it yourself.

Calibration and QC prerequisites

Before you start, make sure all of these are done right

⚖️
Balance calibration
Confirm the balance is accurate with known weights before the formal weighing
🔥
Oven: bring the flasks to constant weight first
Extraction flasks (with boiling chips to prevent bumping) must first be dried at 100–105°C, cooled in a desiccator and weighed, repeating until constant weight, before they count as the “empty flask weight”
🧫
TA control sample (known fat %)
The TA also runs a control sample of known fat content; its recovery is a reference for the quality of today’s overall technique
🧴
Solvent blank
Evaporate the solvent alone, with no sample, and weigh the residue. In theory it should approach 0; it catches contamination from the solvent or glassware
Procedure

Soxhlet apparatus and siphon cycle

6–10 siphon cycles per hour; this class extracts for about 3 hours (a teaching compromise; full extraction often takes 4–6 hours or more)

Loading the sample: Place 2–5 g of dried, finely ground sample in the filter-paper thimble and plug the opening with cotton (to keep the sample from spilling out).
Recovery and drying: After extraction, recover the solvent, dry the extraction flask in a 100–105°C oven, cool it in a desiccator and weigh; repeat until constant weight before the value counts.

Safety rules

Petroleum ether / hexane: highly flammable

🚫
Absolutely no open flames
Petroleum ether/hexane is highly flammable. Heat only with a heating mantle or water bath, and keep open flames out of the room
💨
Work in a fume hood
Weighing, solvent recovery and similar steps are done in the fume hood to reduce vapor inhalation
💧
Check the cooling water at all times
If the condenser water stops, solvent vapor escapes. Check regularly during extraction that the water is flowing
⚠️
Never heat to dryness
Do not evaporate the solvent in the flask completely and then keep heating it dry. Ether-type solvents form peroxides on long standing, which can explode. Collect solvent waste as required and never pour it down the sink (petroleum ether/hexane are not ethers; this warning refers to diethyl ether, as used in Röse–Gottlieb)

Calculation: crude fat %

Weigh the empty flask to constant weight, then weigh again to constant weight after extraction

Crude fat % = W(flask + fat) − W(empty flask)W(sample) × 100
Potato chip example (illustrative data): empty flask (at constant weight) 132.4050 g; flask + fat (constant weight after extraction) 133.4550 g; dried sample 3.0000 g
Fat weight = 133.4550 − 132.4050 = 1.0500 g
Crude fat % (dry-sample basis) = 1.0500/3.0000×100 = 35.00%
Converting back to wet basis: The sample was dried first before going into the thimble, so this 35.00% uses the “dried sample” as the denominator. To compare with the original wet weight, convert using the moisture measured in W2 (2.00%, wet basis):
Crude fat % (wet basis) = 35.00% × (1−2.00%) = 34.30%

Today we also calculate the crude fat % of the blind sample (whole milk powder), but the blind sample goes straight into Soxhlet without hydrolysis. Next you will see why its result reads low.

In-class calculation practice

Pearson Square: how milk fat is “adjusted”

In the opening video, the factory skims the milk first, then adds cream back in proportion to make products with 3.25% / 2% / 0% fat.

Method: write the target % in the middle; along each diagonal, subtract the two numbers and take the absolute value = the “parts” of the ingredient at the other corner; parts ÷ total parts × total weight = kg.

Problem: Mix 36% cream and 0.1% skim milk into 100 kg of 3.25% whole milk. How many kg of each are needed?

How does this relate to today’s fat analysis? Whether you “adjust” how much fat there is or “measure” how much fat there is, it is the same mass-balance logic: the weight (or percentage) of fat must add up before and after mixing/extraction.

Quality control QC · illustrative data

The truth about the blind sample: why does whole milk powder read low by Soxhlet?

Illustrative data, not this class’s actual measurements. The labeled value 26.0% and the reference method Röse–Gottlieb 25.7% nearly agree; direct Soxhlet (no hydrolysis) gives a class mean of only 21.28%, a bias of 4.42 percentage points and a recovery of just 82.8%. The reason: the fat in milk powder is encapsulated by protein and lactose, so the solvent cannot reach it. This is a real systematic error (bias), not an operating mistake. We will return to this result in W8.
Quality control QC

Accuracy vs precision: three checkpoints

🎯
Accuracy: known control sample
The TA’s known-fat control sample (20.0%) reads 19.6% by Soxhlet, a recovery of 98.0%. This confirms that overall technique (solvent, time, temperature) has no obvious bias
📏
Precision: triplicate RSD
Triplicate of the same sample (illustrative): 34.8%, 35.1%, 34.6%, mean 34.83%, RSD ≈ 0.72%. Precision is good, but that does not mean it is accurate (think of the milk powder example)
🧴
Solvent blank catches contamination
Blank residue ≈ 0.0007 g, close to 0, meaning the solvent and glassware are clean. If the blank is high, every sample’s fat % will read too high and must be fixed first
Key point: The milk powder example reminds us that accuracy and precision are two different things. Even if the triplicates are very consistent (good precision), as long as the method itself has a systematic bias (such as extracting directly without hydrolysis), the result is still not accurate.
