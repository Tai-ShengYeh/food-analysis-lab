Spectroscopy crash course

Wavelength (nm), wavenumber (cm⁻¹), energy: three quantities that convert into each other

ν̃ (cm⁻¹) = 10⁷ / λ(nm) E = hc/λ
  • Worked examples: λ=1450 nm → ν̃ ≈ 6897 cm⁻¹; λ=785 nm → ν̃ ≈ 12739 cm⁻¹ (the same light described on a different scale; wavelength and wavenumber run in opposite directions: longer wavelength, smaller wavenumber)
  • Today’s range: near-infrared (NIR) 780–2500 nm ≈ 12800–4000 cm⁻¹; mid-infrared (MIR) 2.5–25 µm (2500–25000 nm) = 4000–400 cm⁻¹. Today’s NIR and the Raman later all live in this conversion system
  • E=hc/λ: h is Planck’s constant, c is the speed of light, λ is the wavelength; the shorter the wavelength (the larger the wavenumber), the higher the photon energy. We will use this when we discuss Raman fluorescence below (a 785 nm photon has lower energy than a 532 nm photon)
Principle

Molecular vibration = a spring: why overtones and combinations exist

  • Think of a chemical bond as a spring: the atoms vibrate back and forth along the bond, but quantum mechanics allows only specific vibrational energy levels (v=0,1,2,3…)
  • The fundamental (v=0→1) signal is the strongest and falls in the mid-infrared (MIR). Real molecules are not perfect springs (anharmonic), so v=0 can also jump directly to v=2 or v=3 with small probability. These are the first overtone (≈2ν), the second overtone (≈3ν), and the combination (ν₁+ν₂) of two vibrational modes added together, which happen to fall in the NIR
  • Each step up makes the signal about 10–100× weaker. That is why NIR absorption is weak, light can penetrate a few millimeters into the sample, and no dilution is needed and the method is nearly non-destructive. X–H bonds (hydrogen is the lightest atom) have the highest vibrational frequencies and the strongest anharmonicity, so their overtones and combinations fall right in the NIR range
What NIR measures

Two measurement modes: transmission vs diffuse reflectance

💧
Transmission (liquid samples)
Light passes straight through the sample; we measure incident light I₀ and transmitted light I:
A = log₁₀(I₀/I) = εbc (the same Beer-Lambert law as the absorbance equation you learned in W07 ↗, just at NIR wavelengths; ε = molar absorptivity, b = path length, c = concentration)
🧂
Diffuse reflectance (powders / opaque samples)
Light hitting a powder surface is scattered repeatedly, and the detector signal is converted to reflectance:
R = (sample − dark) / (white − dark). A powder has no single path length, so apparent absorbance A = log₁₀(1/R) is used in place of true absorbance for chemometric modeling
How to measure “dark” and “white”, and how often, is practiced in “Calibration 2” below.
Principles (brief)

NIR: overtones/combinations vs Raman: inelastic scattering

  • NIR: the signal comes from overtones/combinations of X–H bonds such as O–H, C–H, N–H, e.g. O–H (~1450, ~1940 nm) and C–H (~1150–1210, ~1700–1760 nm). Peaks are broad and overlapping, so quantification always needs chemometrics; water is a very strong absorber and often dominates the spectrum; non-destructive, done in seconds, widely used for real-time production-line testing
  • Raman: the inelastic scattering of laser light by the sample. The wavenumber difference between outgoing and incoming light is the Raman shift (cm⁻¹). The spectrum is sharp like a fingerprint and can be matched directly against a database to identify a substance
  • Water is a very weak Raman scatterer and barely interferes, a big advantage for aqueous samples. The biggest enemy is the fluorescence background (details on the next slide)
  • The virtual state in the diagram is a “non-real” energy level that appears while the laser photon briefly interacts with the molecule. The molecule does not actually stay there; it is just how quantum mechanics describes that instant of interaction
Principle

Two Raman problems: weak signal and fluorescence swamping

🎲
Raman is naturally rare
Only about 1 in every 10⁶–10⁸ incident photons undergoes inelastic (Raman) scattering; nearly all the rest are elastically scattered (Rayleigh) or pass straight through, so the Raman signal is inherently very weak
📉
Intensity ∝ 1/λ⁴
Scattering intensity is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the laser wavelength: (785/532)⁴ ≈ 4.7, so under the same conditions a 532 nm laser gives a Raman signal about 4.7 times that of 785 nm
🔥
Fluorescence = real absorption, then re-emission
Electrons in the sample truly absorb photons and jump to an excited state, then emit broadband fluorescence that can be thousands of times stronger than the Raman signal and completely bury the Raman peaks. It is a completely different phenomenon from Raman scattering
🔻
785 nm photons have lower energy
E=hc/λ: the longer the wavelength, the lower the photon energy, so it is less likely to push electrons to an excited state and therefore less likely to cause fluorescence. This is why dark or fluorescent samples are often switched to 785 nm, even though the signal itself is weaker

Simulated Raman spectra: 532 nm vs 785 nm, same peak positions

Illustrative data / simulated spectra (not measured by this class); the x-axis is evenly spaced Raman shift (cm⁻¹). The 532 nm scattering signal is stronger, but the fluorescence background is also much higher and buries the small peaks; the 785 nm background is flat and the peaks are clear. This is why colored/fluorescent samples are often switched to 785 nm. Dark samples containing conjugated pigments (caramel color, carotenoids, chlorophyll) fluoresce most easily; white crystals and colorless clear liquids interfere little.
Data · simulated spectra

Simulated NIR absorption spectra: water vs ethanol

Illustrative data / simulated spectra, not the specification of any particular instrument. The two water peaks fall near 1450 nm (O–H first overtone) and 1940 nm (O–H combination). Besides a weaker O–H peak, ethanol also shows a C–H first overtone at ~1700–1760 nm and a C–H second overtone at ~1150–1210 nm. This is why the absorbance at a single wavelength can hardly tell the two apart; you need the shape of the whole spectrum (chemometrics).
Bridge

What is chemometrics? — W11–W16 roadmap

  • Chemometrics = using statistical methods to extract information about chemical composition/properties from a large body of (multivariate) spectral numbers
  • A spectrum is just a pile of numbers (intensity vs wavelength/wavenumber); the instrument does not know “this is protein”. You must first measure the true value with a reference method (e.g. Kjeldahl for protein W5 Kjeldahl ↗, titration for acidity W4 Titration ↗), so that a statistical model can learn the relationship between “spectral shape” and “that value”. This is called calibration
  • The next six weeks: preprocessing cleans the spectra → PCA to see what the data look like → PLS to build a quantitative model → VIP / classification / outlier detection → design of experiments (DOE) to plan samples → the mixture-resolution challenge
Calibration 1

Raman x-axis calibration: silicon wafer · polystyrene · cyclohexane

💎
Silicon wafer
A single sharp peak at ~520.7 cm⁻¹; check whether the peak position falls within the instrument’s tolerance — the most common reference for x-axis calibration
🧴
Polystyrene
Main calibration peak 1001.4 cm⁻¹ (ring-breathing mode); 620.9, 1031.8 and 1602.3 cm⁻¹ allow multi-point cross-checks
🧪
Cyclohexane
Peaks such as 801.3 cm⁻¹, used according to the ASTM E1840 Raman shift standards guide
ASTM E1840 = the Raman shift standards guide; it lists accepted peak positions for silicon wafer / polystyrene / cyclohexane etc., so different instruments align to the same wavenumber scale. 532 nm and 785 nm are two separate lasers, so each needs its own x-axis calibration; record laser power and focus (too much power can burn/degrade the sample; poor focus weakens the signal).
Calibration 2a

NIR: dark current · white reference · wavelength check

  • Warm-up: follow the instrument’s recommendation and let the light source and detector stabilize before measuring
  • Dark current: the detector background signal with the light path fully dark; the first step of every calibration
  • White reference: e.g. Spectralon/PTFE (~99% reflectance); redo it every time before using the instrument / before each batch of samples to set the reflectance baseline
  • Wavelength check: use polystyrene or a NIST rare-earth oxide reflectance standard (e.g. the SRM 1920 series, if available) to confirm wavelength accuracy
  • Measure a known spectrum: e.g. water, to confirm the baseline and band shape are normal before measuring unknown samples (a real cautionary example on the next slide)
R = (sample − dark) / (white − dark) A = log₁₀(1/R)
Calibration 2b

A real QC lesson: InnoSpectra cartridge installed the wrong way round

The real lesson: if the 1 mm cuvette cartridge is installed in the normal orientation, the cartridge base blocks the lower light source, so the baseline rises and the expected features almost disappear; only installing it the other way (inverted) gives the correct spectrum. That is exactly why the QC habit is to always measure a known spectrum (e.g. water) first and confirm a normal baseline before trusting any data that follows.
Repeatability

What to look at in replicate scans: SD and SNR

  • SD (standard deviation) — already covered in W01 ↗; it describes how spread out the values are when the same sample is measured repeatedly
  • SNR (signal-to-noise ratio) = signal size ÷ SD of the noise; the larger the SNR, the cleaner the signal relative to the noise
  • The two ends of a spectrum usually have lower light throughput (poorer source/detector efficiency) → weaker signal → usually lower SNR; but whether the absolute SD also rises depends on the noise source and the scale shown, and cannot be inferred directly from “weaker signal”. You will see this in the replicate-scan plot on the next slide
  • Averaging multiple scans reduces noise: averaging n scans reduces random noise to about 1/√n of the original; averaging 5 scans → noise falls to 1/√5 ≈ 1/2.2, an improvement of about 2.2×

Safety rules

Laser safety · solvent handling

🚫
Class 3B/4 laser: never look into the beam
Use goggles or the instrument enclosure as required; with the laser on, do not put your eyes near the beam path
💍
Avoid reflective jewelry when operating the laser
Rings, watches, metal bracelets and other reflective items can cause stray reflections; remove them before you start
🧴
Handle ethanol / acetic acid in a ventilated area
Avoid breathing the vapor or skin contact; cap the cuvettes after use and put them back
🧹
Clean up powder / liquid spills immediately
Handle spills per the lab SOP to avoid cross-contaminating the next group’s measurements
