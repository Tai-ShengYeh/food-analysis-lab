Titratable acidity: manual vs automatic titration

Standardizing NaOH with KHP · phenolphthalein endpoint · PASCO potentiometric titration · digital burette · Bland–Altman method comparison

⏱ 4-hour session🧪 White vinegar, orange juice (our class used citric acid on 11/17; see the record below)📚 Nielsen Food Analysis Ch. 22

中文版 · ← Course home

This week’s questionTwo drinks both have pH 3.5. Is their acidity the same?

pH reflects the activity of free H⁺ in solution, i.e. the acid that is dissociated right now. Titratable acidity is the amount of base a sample uses up when titrated to a specified endpoint, usually expressed as % of the predominant acid (acetic, citric…); it relates to sourness, shelf life and fruit ripeness. This week you measure it by NaOH titration and test whether different titration methods give interchangeable numbers.

After W4 you should be able to:

  • Standardize NaOH with KHP and calculate % acid as N × V × eq. wt / (W × 1000) × 100
  • Read a titration curve: buffer region, half-equivalence point, equivalence point, and three kinds of endpoint (phenolphthalein, specified pH, maximum slope)
  • Run one manual (phenolphthalein) and one PASCO (potentiometric) titration
  • Judge whether two methods agree with a Bland–Altman plot, not just r

Our class record (11/17)

  • What we did: standardized NaOH with KHP, then titrated anhydrous citric acid (2 g/100 mL) using 5, 10 and 20 mL portions. NaOH was delivered with the DLAB dTrite digital burette; the endpoint was faint phenolphthalein pink lasting 30 s.
  • Numbers: the NaOH was made up as 0.1015 N, but KHP standardization gave only 0.0908 N, about 10% low. Each mL of sample used about 3.36–3.40 mL of NaOH; recovery was about 97.5%.

Two comparisons, two different questions

Both labs this week are method comparisons, but they change different things. Work out what changed first; then you know what result to expect.

ComparisonWhat changesWhat stays the sameExpectationWhere
Manual vs PASCOHow the endpoint is detected: phenolphthalein colour vs maximum slope of the pH curve (dpH/dV peak)Same NaOH bottle, same samplesDifferent endpoint definitions can give a systematic biasMain lab
Glass vs dTriteHow NaOH is delivered and volume read: meniscus vs screenEndpoint (both: faint pink lasting 30 s)bias ≈ 0; differences are in precision and handlingSupplementary lab

Both comparisons are read with a Bland–Altman plot: the bias (mean difference) answers “is one method systematically higher or lower?”, the limits of agreement (LoA) answer “how large can a single sample’s difference be?” (when the differences are roughly normal, about 95% of paired differences are expected to fall inside). Both are then compared with a tolerance set in advance. A difference is one % acid minus another, so its unit is percentage points, not a relative percent difference.

Extension: vinegar acidity (stand-alone session)

4 hoursVinegar acidity: ACID181 vs NaOH titration (+ PAL-30S refractometer) → (Chinese only)

The same titratable acidity, measured with two rapid instruments: the ACID181 reads conductivity and the PAL-30S reads refractive index. Using white vinegar, vinegar essence, salt- and sugar-spiked vinegar, black vinegar and balsamic vinegar, students find what fools each of the three methods (salt, sugar, dark colour), then use Bland–Altman plots and the manufacturer's spec bands to judge whether either can replace titration.
