Terms first

Titratable acidity, standardization, N, equivalent weight

🍋
Titratable acidity (TA)
The total amount of acid neutralized by a standard base titrated to a specified end point (phenolphthalein pH 8.2), expressed as % of the predominant acid
⚖️
Standardization and primary standards
NaOH absorbs moisture and CO₂, so its concentration is uncertain; use a pure, stable, non-hygroscopic primary standard (KHP) whose moles are known from its weight to back-calculate its true concentration
🔢
N (normality)
Moles of H⁺ that can be given/accepted per liter; NaOH and HCl exchange one H⁺ each, so N = M
🧮
Equivalent weight
Molecular weight ÷ number of neutralizable H⁺: acetic acid 60.05, lactic acid 90.08, tartaric acid 150.09÷2=75.04, citric acid 192.12÷3=64.04
How to read a titration curve

Six terms

  • Equivalence point: moles of NaOH added = moles of acid in the sample (theoretically exact neutralization)
  • End point: the point at which completion is “seen” experimentally (phenolphthalein color change or dpH/dV peak); a good method makes the end point ≈ the equivalence point
  • pKa: the pH at which a weak acid is half dissociated; acetic acid 4.76
  • Half-equivalence point: when NaOH added is half the equivalence amount, [HA]=[A⁻], and from Henderson–Hasselbalch pH=pKa+log([A⁻]/[HA]) we get pH=pKa
  • Buffer region: the plateau near the half-equivalence point where pH barely changes as base is added
  • Inflection point = steepest part of the curve = equivalence point; the first derivative dpH/dV = how much pH rises per 1 mL added (slope), and its peak is the inflection point

Simulated curve · derivative

Finding the end point in potentiometric titration: dpH/dV peak = inflection point

Same simulated data, finite differences at 0.25 mL spacing; the peak is at V≈18.62 mL with dpH/dV≈8.3, nearly coinciding with the theoretical equivalence point of 18.75 mL. On a real instrument the peak is lower and broader, but its position is still usable — the benefit is that it doesn’t depend on color, so even dark-colored samples get an objective end point.
Calibration before titrating

NaOH standardization · CO₂-free water · pH sensor calibration

Standardize NaOH with KHP: potassium hydrogen phthalate (KHP, MW 204.22, must be dried first) is pure and non-hygroscopic, an ideal primary standard acid — NaOH is strongly hygroscopic and also absorbs CO₂ from air, so every batch must be re-standardized.
Reagents and probe: prepare NaOH with CO₂-free water (boiled or sparged with nitrogen); calibrate the pH sensor in the PASCO software with pH 4/7/9 buffers (two/three points) and apply temperature compensation (electrode slope varies with temperature; the software corrects it automatically) before titrating (Nielsen §22.3.2.2: choose two buffers about 3 pH units apart that bracket the sample; the titration end point is about pH 8.2–8.7, between 7 and 9).
If the system measures volume by drop count: first calibrate “mL per drop” by the gravimetric method (same approach as W1); drop count itself is not a volume unit.
Dilute samples with CO₂-free water too: dissolved CO₂ forms carbonic acid, consuming extra NaOH and making the measured % acid too high.
Titration procedure

Manual Titration vs PASCO Automatic Titration

Manual: add 0.1 N NaOH from a burette; a persistent pink with phenolphthalein is the end point; each sample in triplicate. PASCO: motorized burette + pH probe wirelessly send the pH–volume curve to a laptop in real time and project it, so the whole class watches the curve form together.

In-class activity

Predict–Observe–Explain

  • Before PASCO starts titrating, each group first sketches its predicted pH–volume curve on paper
  • Add a few drops of phenolphthalein to the PASCO beaker too — during titration, at the moment you see it turn pink, mark that point on the projected real-time curve
  • Compare this mark with the inflection point calculated by the software: do they coincide?
Vinegar (a clean weak acid): near the equivalence point the curve rises almost vertically, and the phenolphthalein color change almost coincides with the inflection point (theoretically less than one drop apart).

Red wine (many organic acids + color): the curve climbs more gently, the equivalence point is less distinct, and the red color masks the phenolphthalein pink, so the visual end point is harder to judge and seen later than the inflection point — which is exactly why dark samples need potentiometric titration.

Calculation: from titrant volume to % acid

Standardize NaOH first, then calculate the sample’s % acid

NNaOH = m(KHP)204.22 × V(L)
% acid = N × V × equivalent weightW × 1000 × 100
KHP standardization example: weigh 0.5105 g KHP, titration uses 24.85 mL NaOH
N = 0.5105 / (204.22 × 0.02485) = 0.1006 M
Vinegar example: take 2.00 mL vinegar, uses 15.1 mL of 0.1006 M NaOH (acetic acid equivalent weight 60.05)
% acid = 0.1006×15.1×60.05/(2.00×1000)×100 ≈ 4.56%

Yogurt is sampled by weight (not volume) — same formula, but W is the sample “grams” and the result is reported as % (w/w) rather than % (w/v); lactic acid equivalent weight 90.08. Citrus/red wine → citric acid (64.04)/tartaric acid (75.04); always expressed as the predominant acid.

Method-comparison QC · reading & uses

Bland–Altman: four reading steps and applications

① Set the tolerance first
Decide “how big a difference is acceptable” from the intended use or regulations before seeing the data, so you cannot loosen it afterwards.
② Check the bias
A paired t-test asks whether the mean difference is significantly ≠ 0; a constant bias can be corrected.
③ Check the LoA
Only when the whole LoA lies within the tolerance are the methods interchangeable; a wide LoA = individual differences are unstable.
④ Check the shape
Flat band = good; slope = proportional bias (check volume/concentration calibration); funnel = error grows with level (use % differences); outlier = go back to the raw records.

Method-comparison QC

Manual vs PASCO: Bland–Altman plot

  • Paired design: for the same sample, measure once with each of manual and PASCO, and plot one point per sample: x = mean of the two methods, y = difference between them
  • Bias = mean difference = 0.036% (illustrative data, PASCO − manual)
  • Limits of agreement (LoA) = bias ± 1.96×SD = [-0.099, 0.170]: 95% of between-method differences are expected to fall within it; the two methods are interchangeable only if this width is acceptable in practice (SD=0.068, n=12)
  • Paired t-test (tests whether the mean difference is nonzero): t=1.80 (df=11) < critical value 2.20 → no significant systematic bias; but the red wine points are clearly more scattered
Method-comparison QC (2)

High r ≠ the two methods agree

Why not look only at the correlation coefficient r? The correlation coefficient r (−1 to 1, how much the two rise and fall together) is 0.999 in this illustrative data set (nearly perfect correlation!), yet the two methods can still be biased, and agreement for red wine is clearly worse — r only looks at “whether the two move together,” not “how big the difference is or how stable it is,” which is exactly why Bland–Altman exists.
Back to the two opening questions

pH vs titratable acidity; pasteurization vs sterilization

🧪
Why measure both pH and titratable acidity?
  • pH = how much free H⁺ is in the solution → determines whether microbes can grow and how strong a heat treatment is needed
  • Titratable acidity = the total amount of all acid that can be neutralized by base (including undissociated weak acid) → relates to sourness intensity, fermentation progress, and quality specifications
  • The two are not interchangeable: proteins, phosphates and organic-acid salts in food buffer pH. Two drinks with the same pH 3.5 can differ several-fold in titratable acidity, and taste different in sourness
🔥
Pasteurization vs sterilization
  • Pasteurization: about 63 °C for 30 min or 72 °C for 15 s, kills pathogens and most vegetative cells; the product still needs refrigeration
  • Sterilization: UHT at about 135–150 °C for a few seconds, or canning at 121 °C under pressure, kills even bacterial spores → commercial sterility, shelf-stable at room temperature. The video’s 130–150 °C falls in this category
  • Relation to pH: low-acid foods (pH > 4.6 and aw > 0.85) must use conditions that can destroy botulinum spores; acidic foods can use milder heating — this is one reason factories measure pH

In industry slang, UHT is also often called “ultra-high-temperature pasteurization”; in reports and regulatory documents, please use precise terms.
