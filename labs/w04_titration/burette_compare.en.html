Glass Burette vs Digital Burette: Same Endpoint, a Different Way to Measure Volume

1. What are we comparing?

The main W4 lab compares two ways of detecting the endpoint: phenolphthalein colour change (by eye) vs potentiometric titration (the inflection point of the pH curve). Because the endpoints differ, the two methods can show a systematic bias.

Today is different: both burettes use phenolphthalein and stop at the same pink endpoint. The only thing that changes is how the NaOH is delivered and how its volume is read:

Glass burette (50 mL, Class A)

  • Deliver by turning the stopcock; read the meniscus by eye
  • Smallest division 0.1 mL; estimate to 0.02 mL
  • Error sources: parallax, meniscus reading, drop size, liquid left on the wall

Digital burette (DLAB dTrite)

  • Mounts directly on the NaOH reagent bottle; piston delivery
  • Volume shown on a display; catalogue resolution 0.01 mL
  • Error sources: piston mechanics, air bubbles in the line, not zeroing

Expected resultBecause the endpoint is the same, the mean difference (bias) between the two should be close to 0. If your Bland–Altman plot is a flat band with no significant bias, that is not "nothing happened". It is exactly what this lab sets out to show: the two burettes are interchangeable.

What is really worth looking at: (1) how accurately each burette delivers (gravimetric check); (2) how much the precision of repeat titrations (RSD) differs; (3) whether the limits of agreement (LoA) fall inside the tolerance you set in advance.

2. Learning objectives

  1. Calibrate both burettes gravimetrically (the W1 method), calculate systematic error and CV, and compare them with the catalogue specifications.
  2. Standardize NaOH against KHP with each burette, then measure the titratable acidity of white vinegar and orange juice.
  3. Derive a sensible tolerance from "the volume of one drop" and "the reading error" before looking at any data.
  4. Use a relative-difference Bland–Altman plot and a paired t-test to judge whether the methods are interchangeable, and compare precision using RSD.
  5. Explain why "standardizing and titrating with the same burette" cancels a proportional volume error.

5. Set the tolerance first (before seeing any data)

The first step in reading a Bland–Altman plot is to decide in advance how large a difference between the methods is acceptable, so you are not tempted to loosen the standard after seeing the results. Use two quantities:

(a) The effect of one drop. One drop of NaOH is about 0.05 mL. The samples need about 12–15 mL of NaOH, so a one-drop difference gives a relative error of about

0.05 / 14.9 ≈ 0.34% (vinegar)　　0.05 / 12.7 ≈ 0.39% (orange juice)

As % acetic acid in vinegar: 0.1 × 0.05 × 60.05 / (2.00 × 1000) × 100 ≈ 0.015% (vinegar is about 4.5%).

(b) Reading error of the glass burette. The start and end readings are each estimated to ±0.02 mL, so the worst case combined is 0.04 mL:

0.04 / 14.9 ≈ 0.27% (vinegar)　　0.04 / 12.7 ≈ 0.31% (orange juice)

This handout's exampleFor both samples, "one drop" is roughly a 0.3–0.4% relative error, so comparing relative differences is the most convenient. The example uses tolerance = ±1% (relative difference, about 3 drops).

Your group may choose a different number, but you must write down your reasoning, and do it before you start titrating.

6. Part A　Gravimetric check of both burettes

Same principle as W1: mass of water × Z factor = volume actually delivered. Look up Z (mL/g) by water temperature (ISO 8655-6, 1013 hPa):

Water temp (°C)181920212223242526

Procedure

  1. Measure and record the water temperature. Let the water, burettes and weighing bottles all equilibrate to room temperature first.
  2. Glass burette: fill with deionized water, clear the bubble from the tip, set near 0.00 and read the start (to 0.02 mL). Deliver about 10 mL into a tared weighing bottle, wait 30 s for the wall to drain, read the end, then weigh. Repeat 10 times (refill each time).
  3. dTrite: purge bubbles and zero according to the manual, deliver a displayed 10.00 mL into a tared weighing bottle, and weigh. Repeat 10 times.
  4. Note: Part A uses water, so the dTrite must be mounted on a water bottle first; afterwards, move it back to the NaOH bottle and purge bubbles again. If there is only one dTrite and it is already on the NaOH bottle, ask the TA to complete Part A with water before class.

Calculations

Vi = mi × Z　　Systematic error = mean(V) − nominal volume　　CV = SD(V) / mean(V) × 100%

For the glass burette the "nominal volume" is each reading difference (end − start); for the dTrite it is the displayed 10.00 mL.

7. Part B　KHP standardization (2 runs per burette)

  1. Weigh about 0.50 g of dried KHP (to 0.1 mg) into an Erlenmeyer flask, dissolve in about 50 mL of CO₂-free water, and add 2–3 drops of phenolphthalein.
  2. Titrate with the glass burette to a faint pink that persists for 30 s; read to 0.02 mL.
  3. Weigh a second portion of KHP and titrate it with the dTrite; record the displayed volume (0.01 mL).
  4. Do 2 runs with each burette. Each burette gets its own N, and Part C uses each burette's own N. Report N to 5 decimal places: the difference being compared here is only 0.1–0.2%, and rounding to 4 places would hide it.

8. Part C　Sample titrations (vinegar, orange juice × 3 × 2 burettes)

  1. Vinegar: pipette 2.00 mL into an Erlenmeyer flask; add about 50 mL of CO₂-free water and 2–3 drops of phenolphthalein.
  2. Orange juice: pipette 10.00 mL; add about 50 mL of CO₂-free water and 3 drops of phenolphthalein. (The juice colour makes the endpoint harder to see; keep a flask of the same sample without base as a colour reference.)
  3. Take 6 aliquots of each sample: 3 for the glass burette and 3 for the dTrite.
  4. Alternate the order (glass → dTrite → glass → dTrite …) so that "getting better with practice" does not bias whichever burette goes second.
  5. Use the same endpoint for both: faint pink persisting for 30 s. Near the endpoint, slow both burettes down to drop-by-drop (or smaller) additions.
% acid = N × V(mL) × eq. wt / (W × 1000) × 100　　vinegar as acetic acid 60.05, orange juice as citric acid 64.04

9. Data analysis

9.1 Precision: RSD

Calculate the RSD of the 3 replicates for each group, sample and burette. When pooling the class, use the root mean square: RSDpooled = √(mean of the groups' RSD²).

9.2 Agreement: relative-difference Bland–Altman

Each group gives one pair of results per sample (glass mean, dTrite mean): 6 groups × 2 samples = 12 pairs for the class. Always define the difference as dTrite − glass.

x = (glass + dTrite) / 2　　relative difference y = (dTrite − glass) / x × 100%
bias = mean(y)　　LoA = bias ± 1.96 × SD(y)

Why relative differences? Vinegar is about 4.5% and orange juice about 0.8%, so the absolute differences differ several-fold in size and would form a funnel on the same plot. Section 5 already showed that "one drop" is about 0.3–0.4% for both samples, so relative differences put them on the same scale.

9.3 Systematic bias: paired t-test

t = bias / (SD(y) / √n)　　df = n − 1

With n = 12, df = 11 and the two-tailed α = 0.05 critical value is t = 2.201. If |t| is below the critical value, there is no significant systematic bias.

Tools: the "5_手動vs自動" (manual vs automatic) sheet in EX04 runs the same Bland–Altman, paired t-test and F-test; enter the glass burette as "manual" and the dTrite as "automatic". Or use the Bland–Altman section of NB04. Note that both default to absolute differences; add your own column for relative differences.

Example conclusions

  • Precision: pooled RSD, vinegar glass 0.20% vs dTrite 0.14%; orange juice glass 0.33% vs dTrite 0.25%. The dTrite has the smaller RSD in 9 of the 12 group–sample combinations.
  • Relative-difference Bland–Altman (n = 12): bias = +0.035%, SD = 0.187%, LoA = −0.33% to +0.40%.
  • Paired t: t = 0.65 < 2.201 (df = 11), p = 0.53 → no significant systematic bias.
  • Verdict: the whole LoA lies within the ±1% set in advance → over the concentration range of these two samples, the two burettes are interchangeable.

Notice the gap between Part A and Part C: for delivery alone, the dTrite's CV is only 1/3 of the glass burette's (0.054% vs 0.18%), yet in real titrations the RSD only drops from 0.20% to 0.14%. That is because both burettes share the variability of judging the phenolphthalein endpoint; better reading is only part of the total error.

11. Discussion questions

  1. Why does this lab expect bias ≈ 0? Give two handling mistakes that would push the bias clearly away from 0. (Hint: bubbles, parallax, using the wrong N.)
  2. In the example, Part A shows the glass burette reading about 0.14% more than it delivers and the dTrite delivering about 0.10% more than it displays, and in Part B the two N values differ by about 0.2%. Why do the Part C % acid values still agree? If you instead "standardize the NaOH with the glass burette and titrate samples with the dTrite", would % acid come out high or low? By roughly how much?
  3. Why does a 3-fold difference in CV in Part A shrink to a much smaller difference in RSD in Part C?
  4. The correlation coefficient r for these data will be very close to 1. Why is r almost meaningless here? (Hint: there are only two concentration levels.)
  5. If the sample were red wine, would the dTrite solve the problem of a hard-to-see endpoint? What method should you use instead?
  6. Apart from the data, what are the pros and cons of a digital burette in terms of safety, operating time and reagent use?

12. Safety and dTrite care

  • NaOH is corrosive: wear goggles and gloves throughout; if it gets on skin or in the eyes, rinse immediately with plenty of water and tell the TA.
  • Phenolphthalein is an ethanol solution: flammable; keep away from flames and cap it after use.
  • Do not leave NaOH in the piston and lines for long: NaOH makes glass and pistons seize. At the end of class, flush the dTrite with deionized water according to the manual, and empty it if it will not be used for a long time. Rinse the glass burette and stopcock immediately as well.
  • CO₂: keep the NaOH bottle open as briefly as possible; fit a CO₂ absorption tube (soda lime) if you have one.
  • Chemical compatibility: before using the dTrite with other reagents (e.g. AgNO₃, organic solvents), check the manufacturer's compatibility table.
  • Do not let the piston draw air: when the reagent bottle is nearly empty, stop and change bottles to avoid drawing in air and creating bubbles.
