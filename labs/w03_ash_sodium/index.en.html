Himalayan pink salt ads claim “84 minerals”
and sell it for about 20× the price of regular table salt — is that true?

Pink salt is actually 96–99% NaCl; at a typical intake of about 5 g of salt per day, the trace minerals contribute almost nothing; and pink salt is usually not iodized.

Principle (1): Dry Ashing

Ash = total mineral residue

  • Pre-char the sample on a hot plate to drive off most of the organic matter, so it doesn’t spatter or catch fire in the furnace
  • Move into the muffle furnace and ash at 550°C (typical range 525–600°C) until white or light gray
  • Ash is the total weight of all inorganic residue in the sample (a mixture of oxides, carbonates, etc.), not the amount of any single mineral
  • Cool in a desiccator to avoid moisture uptake and weight gain; repeat ashing + weighing until constant weight

Principle (2): Na⁺ Ion-Selective Electrode (ISE)

Potential ↔ log(activity): the Nernst relationship

Nernst relationship: electrode potential is proportional to the logarithm of Na⁺ activity — each 10-fold change in activity shifts the potential by 59.2 mV (25°C, monovalent ion). The reference junction provides a fixed comparison potential. So before use, do a two-point calibration with two standards of known concentration to establish the “potential vs log concentration” line, and the meter then converts the sample potential to concentration (the W4 pH meter works on the same principle).

Principle (3)

Activity vs concentration: why dilute?

🧲
Activity
The “effective” concentration of an ion: a = γ·C (γ = activity coefficient ≤ 1). With many ions present they hinder each other and γ falls; in dilute solutions γ ≈ 1, so activity ≈ concentration
🌊
Ionic strength
An index of the total ions in solution (weighted by charge); soy sauce and soup powders are very high → γ small → readings low
⚖️
Ionic strength adjuster (ISA)
Formal ISE methods add equal amounts of ISA to standards and samples so γ is the same; pen meters usually skip it and instead use dilution to bring the sample’s ionic strength close to the standards’ and back into the measuring range
⚠️
Selectivity and interference
Cations such as K⁺ also respond slightly → readings run high; check the manual for the range
Why no Mohr titration this year?

Safety, and Na⁺ ≠ NaCl

☠️
Safety
AgNO₃ is corrosive/oxidizing and stains skin; the K₂CrO₄ indicator contains hexavalent chromium (a carcinogen) and also generates chromium/silver waste; the pen-type Na⁺ ISE is safer and faster
🔀
Na⁺ ≠ NaCl
Mohr titration measures Cl⁻, while the ISE measures Na⁺ — additives such as MSG, baking soda (NaHCO₃) and sodium benzoate also contain sodium, so converting all Na directly to “salt” will overestimate the NaCl content

Calibration: two points + drift monitoring

Follow the rules before and after measuring

  • Do a two-point calibration with the two standards supplied by the manufacturer
  • After about every 10 samples, re-measure a standard to confirm there is no drift
  • Before each new sample, rinse the electrode and gently blot it dry with lens tissue to avoid carry-over contamination
  • Measure at the same temperature whenever possible (temperature affects ion activity and potential)
Why monitor drift?
With long use or a fouled membrane surface, readings slowly shift; if the re-measured standard is off by too much, recalibrate and check whether samples measured “after the drift” need to be re-measured.

Safety rules

High temperature, crucibles, no silver/chromium waste

🔥
Muffle furnace
At 550°C, handle only with long-handled crucible tongs, wear heat-resistant gloves, and wear a face shield/goggles when opening the furnace
🧊
Crucible cooling
Place the ashed crucible in the desiccator to cool before taking it out to weigh; never touch a hot crucible with bare hands
🧪
Hot-plate pre-char
Work in a fume hood to prevent charring smoke from accumulating
✅
No silver/chromium waste this year
Using the Na⁺ ISE instead of AgNO₃ and K₂CrO₄ lowers the risk from corrosive chemicals and carcinogenic heavy-metal waste

Ash calculation: worked example

Ash % = ash weight ÷ sample weight × 100

Ash % = ash weightsample weight × 100
  • Empty crucible W₀ = 26.184 g
  • Crucible + sample = 28.696 g → sample weight = 2.512 g
  • Crucible + ash after ashing (constant weight) = 26.221 g → ash weight = 0.037 g
  • Ash % = 0.037 ÷ 2.512 × 100 ≈ 1.47%

Na calculation: from reading to conversion

C = reading × dilution factor; NaCl equivalent = Na × 2.542

Csample = reading × dilution factor
NaCl equivalent = Na × 2.542
(58.44 ÷ 22.99 ≈ 2.542)
  • Potato chip prep: grind 5.00 g + 100 mL hot deionized water, stir 10 min, filter, and make up to volume
  • The filtrate (not diluted further) measured directly with the ISE gives 380 ppm (mg/L) Na
  • Total Na in 100 mL filtrate = 380 × 0.1 = 38 mg
  • Convert back to the 5.00 g sample → Na per 100 g sample = 38 ÷ 5.00 × 100 = 760 mg/100 g
  • Compare with the “sodium mg per serving” on the package nutrition label and discuss possible reasons for the difference
  • NaCl equivalent = the amount of salt equivalent if all the Na were present as NaCl (not the same as the “equivalence point” in W4 titration)

Quality control (QC): standard addition recovery, drift, dilution error, blank

How do we know today’s data are reliable?

Recovery % = Cspiked − CunspikedCadded × 100

Standard addition recovery (spike recovery): add a known amount of Na⁺ standard to the sample and measure again; compare the readings before and after spiking to see how much is recovered, which checks method accuracy (≠ the “standard addition method” used for calibration); in teaching, 90–110% is often taken as the acceptable range.

Other QC points:
• Drift: re-measure a standard every 10 samples
• Dilution factor error: pipettes/volumetric flasks must be accurate, and don’t use the wrong factor in the conversion
• Blank: check with deionized water that the baseline is near 0
