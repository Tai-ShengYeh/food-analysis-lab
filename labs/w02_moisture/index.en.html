Spot the error first

The beef jerky factory video says:
500 g fresh beef → 100 g jerky?

Video: Beef jerky factory video →

Before doing the experiment, use a mass balance to check whether this number is reasonable — this is practice, not mocking the video.

Your task: lean beef is about 73% moisture (about 27% solids);
if the finished jerky is about 20% moisture, what should the yield be? Roughly how many grams of jerky can 500 g of fresh beef give?
Mass balance:
Solids are hardly lost during drying, so they are the “invariant”.
Yield = solids fraction (fresh meat) ÷ solids fraction (product)
= 0.27 ÷ 0.80 ≈ 34%
500 g × 34% ≈ 170 g, not 100 g.

An even bigger contradiction: 500 g of fresh beef already contains 500×27% ≈ 135 g of solids — even if you dried the water down to 0%, you could not end up with only 100 g. (Commercial jerky also has sugar, soy sauce, and other marinade added, so the yield would only be higher.)

Principle 1

How moisture content is defined and measured

Wet basis % = Wwet − WdryWwet × 100
Dry basis % = Water weightDry solids weight × 100
🔥
105°C atmospheric oven
Dry to constant weight (two consecutive “dry → cool → weigh” cycles differing by ≤ 2 mg) to show the moisture has been fully removed; the most common and most fundamental method
🌡️
Vacuum oven
High-sugar samples undergo the Maillard reaction at 105°C, producing water and volatiles themselves that are miscounted as moisture; for such easily decomposed samples, use a vacuum oven at lower temperature and low oxygen
Handling reminders: after drying, cool in a desiccator (a sealed jar containing desiccant) before weighing (to avoid moisture uptake); grind the sample to a uniform powder.
Infrared (IR) moisture analyzer: a heating lamp plus a built-in balance gives a result in a few minutes; fast, but it must first be verified (calibrated) against the oven method before its readings can be trusted. 📖 Full principles deck (Nielsen Ch15) →
Principles 2

Water activity aw: not the same as moisture %

aw = p / p₀ (p: water vapor pressure above the food; p₀: saturated vapor pressure of pure water at the same temperature; = equilibrium relative humidity ÷ 100)

The same moisture content (%) does not mean the same water activity — the water may be free water, or it may be bound to solutes or structure (bound water); the more bound, the lower the aw. (The curve relating moisture % to aw for each food is called the moisture sorption isotherm.) aw changes with temperature (the same food has different aw at different temperatures); food composition and capillary pore structure also affect how tightly water is bound. 📖 Full principles deck (Nielsen Ch15) →

Typical lower limits for microbial growth (textbook values)
  • 🦠 Most bacteria: aw ≈ 0.91
  • 🍞 Most yeasts: aw ≈ 0.88
  • 🍄 Most molds: aw ≈ 0.80
  • ⚠️ Xerophilic molds: aw ≈ 0.61

Calibration 1

aw meter: calibrate with saturated salt solutions

Saturated salt solutionaw (25°C, Greenspan 1977)
LiCl0.113
MgCl₂0.328
NaCl0.753
K₂SO₄0.973
  • Why it works as a standard: in a sealed container, the air above a saturated salt solution (undissolved crystals still at the bottom of the cup) reaches a fixed relative humidity; at 25°C, NaCl gives a fixed 75.3%, so the aw meter should read 0.753 for it
  • Procedure: put the salt standard in the sample cup → close the measuring chamber → wait for the reading to stabilize (equilibrate) → compare with the table value
  • Choose 2–4 salts that span the sample range (potato chips low, beef jerky mid)
  • Acceptance: the difference is within the manufacturer's specification; standard and sample at the same temperature

Data processing

Wet basis vs dry basis: a worked example

Empty pan 25.1234 g; pan + sample 28.1234 g; after drying (pan + dry sample) 27.9734 g

  • Sample weight = 28.1234 − 25.1234 = 3.0000 g
  • Water weight = 28.1234 − 27.9734 = 0.1500 g
  • Dry solids weight = 3.0000 − 0.1500 = 2.8500 g
Wet basis % = 0.1500 / 3.0000 × 100 = 5.00%

Dry basis % = 0.1500 / 2.8500 × 100 = 5.26%

The denominators differ: wet basis divides by the “original wet weight”, dry basis by the “dry solids weight” — with the same data, the two percentages are not the same.

Extension · real research data

Moisture sorption isotherm: can the monolayer water be calculated?

At different aw values, connect the dry-basis moisture content of the same food into one curve and you get a moisture sorption isotherm (MSI). Fitting with the BET/GAB models can, in theory, give the monolayer water Xm — the moisture content at which the food is most stable.
