Food Analysis Lab · For teachers

Learning Assessment

Students use different tools (Excel, Orange, Colab), so assessment should look at judgment and conclusions, not software skills. This page is a ready-to-use assessment design: pre/post tests show how much was learned over the semester, weekly exit tickets and homework feedback show where students get stuck, and practical exams and the project show whether they can independently analyze data they have not seen.

Four principles

Align with goalsEach learning goal needs at least two different kinds of evidence, and assessment items map directly to goals.
Tool-neutralOne rubric scores work in all three tools; students who get it right in Excel earn full marks, and Colab is only a bonus.
Formative first, summative laterWeekly feedback is ungraded or low-stakes so students dare to make mistakes; grading is concentrated on reports, practical exams, and the project.
Data-informed teachingPre/post tests and item analysis reveal concepts the whole class has not mastered, to adjust next semester.

Total score = Σ(level × weight) ÷ 4. Toolkit sheet “8_Rubrics” has a calculator and also the practical-exam rubric (with one extra item, “cross-check”: verify 1 value a second way). Give the rubric to students in the first week.

How to set the practical exams

W9 midterm: method comparison or proficiency test

  1. Give an unseen long-format dataset (e.g., manual vs. automatic acetic acid titration for 6 groups, or blind-sample ash for 5 groups).
  2. Task: descriptive statistics → find outliers → choose the right test (paired t / Bland–Altman or ANOVA) → write a 3-sentence conclusion.
  3. Grading focus: choose the right method and give the reason, correct values, and a conclusion that answers the question.
  4. Tools: Excel (open book; students may use their own EX04, EX08) or Colab.

W18 final: spectral modeling

  1. Give a set of spectra and reference values, with the calibration and test sets already labeled.
  2. Task: preprocessing → PCA score plot and interpret one cluster → choose LV for PLS → report RMSECV, RMSEP, RPD → state the applicability domain.
  3. Grading focus: grouped cross-validation, drawing conclusions from RMSEP rather than R², and naming the model’s limitations.
  4. Tools: Orange (students may adapt OR13, OR16) or Colab.
You can prepare two versions of the data (Version A and B) so neighbors get different ones; work out the answers beforehand in the Colab notebooks and grade against the toolkit rubric.

How to tell whether students improved

1. Normalized gain g

g = (post − pre) ÷ (max − pre). For the class: <g> = (post mean − pre mean) ÷ (max − pre mean)

It means “how much of what was not yet known got learned.” Hake (1998) levels: g < 0.3 low, 0.3–0.7 medium, > 0.7 high. Compare the class <g> with previous years to see whether teaching changes worked; also check the paired t-test p-value and effect size (Cohen's d) to confirm the improvement is not by chance.

2. Item analysis

  • Proportion correct P: items still below 0.5 on the post-test are concepts the class has not mastered; strengthen that week next semester.
  • Discrimination r (correlation of the item score with the total score): items below 0.2 may be unclear and need rewriting.
  • Summarize by learning goal (CLO1–CLO3) to see which strand is weakest.

3. Confidence vs. ability

High self-efficacy but a wrong answer on the matching test item means students “think they know it”; these misconceptions most need direct attention in class.

4. End-of-semester review

After the semester, spend 30 minutes with toolkit sheets “5_Learning gains” and “6_Item analysis” and write down 3 things to adjust next semester (e.g., “add one more residual-plot exercise in W7”), then add them to the semester plan.

Sample pre/post test questions

The full 15 questions (covering W1–W16 topics) are in toolkit sheet “2_Pre/post item bank”, with answers and linked goals, ready to paste into Google Forms. Questions test concepts only, so students can answer them whichever tool they use.

See 5 sample questions
1. (W1) Repeating the same pipette 10 times gives a very small SD, but the mean is 0.03 mL below the nominal value (Class A tolerance ±0.02 mL). This means?
Precise but not accurate / Accurate and precise / Accurate but not precise / Neither accurate nor precise
2. (W2/W4) Two methods measure the same set of samples. To compare the methods, you should use?
Paired t-test or Bland–Altman plot / Independent two-sample t-test / Look only at the correlation coefficient r / Compare only the two SDs
3. (W7) A calibration curve has R² = 0.999. What does this mean?
The residual plot may still be curved; check the residuals too / It is definitely linear / LOD is definitely low / Sample concentrations are definitely accurate
4. (W8) The specification is protein ≥ 25.0%, and the measured value is 25.2 ± 0.4% (U, k = 2). What should be concluded?
Cannot decide: the interval crosses the specification limit / Release / Do not release / Measure once more and average, then release
5. (W13) In cross-validation, the 3 replicates of the same sample are split between the training and validation sets. RMSECV will be?
Too low (over-optimistic) / Too high / Unchanged / Impossible to calculate
